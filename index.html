VSL Script: "The One-Time Medicare Decision"

Property: Emily / The Medicare Coach Format: Medium VSL, about 14 minutes (roughly 2,100 spoken words at Emily's pace). Emily on camera, one seated setup, kitchen-table register. Cutaway cards and simple motion graphics are marked [ON SCREEN] and B-roll is marked [B-ROLL]. Feeds: vsl-funnel/index.html. Every CTA goes to vsl-funnel/enroll.html, which hands off to the live checkout. Source: Every rule, story and number below comes from Emily's own 10-16-2026 live workshop (transcript in ../2026-10-16 - Webinar Transcript/webinar.txt) or from brand/. Nothing was invented. The items Emily still needs to confirm are listed at the bottom.

Strategy (built with the vsl-master-architect skill)

Awareness: problem-aware. These viewers know Medicare is confusing. They don't know the decision is mostly one-time, and they don't know the advice they're getting is shaped by commissions. Sophistication: stage 4. Every insurer says "we make Medicare easy." The way to stand apart is the mechanism, plus the fact that Emily is not an agent. Lead type: a qualifier lead combined with a contrarian lead (creative direction lock, move 1). The asset threshold is the hook.

16-word core:

Medicare's one-time "golden ticket" window is the key to protecting your doctors and your savings, and it's only used right through a 100% independent, doctor-first decision process.

Problem mechanism, the "Golden Ticket Window": for the first six months you can join any supplement plan regardless of your health. After that, companies can ask about your health and turn you down. Most people only find out they're in the wrong plan once they're sick, and by then it's too late to switch. On top of that, the advice most people get is paid for by the plans that pay the most commission. (The four moves from the lock: wealth qualifier, the enemy is the incentive and not the person, a dual stake, and irreversibility as the urgency.)

Solution mechanism, "The Five-Step Medicare Decision Process": Emily's own framework, which she says has been used with thousands of people: 1. When to join 2. Doctor-first program choice (Original Medicare or Advantage) 3. Part D 4. The right supplement letter, then the right company 5. Make it official

Offer: the Medicare Enrollment Concierge. One time: $1,497, or 2 x $749. A spouse adds $700. Bonuses: first money-saving annual review, Medicare timeline, physician network research, and a 12-month help desk.

3. THE PROBLEM MECHANISM: THE GOLDEN TICKET WINDOW (3:45 to 6:15)

[ON SCREEN: animated timeline. A green "6-MONTH WINDOW" bar starts at "Your Part B starts". After it ends, the bar turns grey and a padlock icon appears.]

So here's the rule. Original Medicare usually covers about 80% of approved costs. The other 20% is on you, unless you have a Medicare supplement plan, which some people call Medigap, and 20% of uncovered costs can get very expensive, very quickly.

When your Medicare Part B starts, you get a six-month window where you can join any supplement plan you want, no matter your health. You could have cancer, diabetes, heart issues, anything, and they have to accept you and they can't charge you more for it. I call it a golden ticket.

But once that window closes, in most states those companies can ask about your health history, and they can turn you down.

[ON SCREEN: "Easy: Original Medicare → Advantage later. Hard, if not impossible: Advantage → Original Medicare later."]

That's why it's much easier to start on Original Medicare and switch to Medicare Advantage later than to go the other way. If you start on Advantage and later want to come back, in most cases you'd better be healthy. Most people don't realize they're in the wrong plan until they're sick, and by then it's usually too late to switch.

Now, here's why nobody's telling you this.

[ON SCREEN: "Who usually calls you first?" with icons for phone, mailbox and TV.]

Usually it's an insurance company that reaches out to you, and most of the time they'll encourage you to join a Medicare Advantage plan. That's not because anyone is a bad person. Many agents are really helpful. It's that these plans make insurance companies the most money, so they spend more on advertising, they pay their agents more to sell them, and they add perks like gym memberships and $0 premiums to bring you in. What they rarely talk about is which doctors and hospitals you can or can't see.

[B-ROLL: hospital exterior with no logos, wide shot. ON SCREEN: "Many top research hospitals accept Original Medicare, but very few to no Advantage plans."]

When my dad and I were going through this, he was very healthy. But we wanted a plan that would still work for him in the worst case, and if something serious ever happened, we wanted him to have the option to go to a place like MD Anderson. So we checked, and MD Anderson accepted Original Medicare but only one Advantage plan out of hundreds, and that one was only for people in the Houston area. We see the same thing with places like Mayo Clinic and Cleveland Clinic. They usually take Original Medicare and very few to no Advantage plans.

And here's where your savings come in. If you didn't get a supplement plan when you first joined, and then you get sick, it's usually too late to get one. So Medicare covers its 80%, and the other 20% of every test and every treatment comes out of your savings. None of us know what our health future holds, but with the right supplement plan we can at least control the financial burden of what it does hold.

[ON SCREEN, dual-stake fork graphic: left path "Your savings → your family, travel, your plans" / right path "Your savings → avoidable medical bills"]

So if you have savings, this matters twice: it decides whether you can see the doctors you want, and it decides whether your savings go to the life you planned or to medical bills you could have avoided. The more you've saved, the more you have exposed.

4. THE SOLUTION MECHANISM: THE FIVE-STEP MEDICARE DECISION PROCESS (6:15 to 9:00)

[ON SCREEN: five numbered cards, revealed one at a time.]

So how do you get it right? Here are the same five steps we follow for every client.

Step 1: Know when to join. By 65 you need to be on Medicare or have acceptable coverage, or you can face lifetime penalties. I recommend applying for Part A and B about three months before you want it to start. A few months ago we helped Bob, who applied on his own about five days before his birthday month. Medicare made a mistake on his file and started rejecting his doctor's bills. And if you're working past 65, just because you can delay Medicare doesn't always mean you should. We helped Jane, who planned to work until 68, and our research found she could save $18,000 in her first year by leaving her employer plan and joining Medicare instead.

Step 2: Choose your program, doctors first. Before anything else, make a list of your doctors and ask each one two questions: what type of Medicare do you accept, and are there any rules I should know about, like a limit on Medicare patients. Most people fit clearly into one program or the other just from their doctors.

Step 3: Get the right Part D drug plan, every year. These plans look alike, but small details make big cost differences, and they change every single year. We've helped one client, Mary, review her plan every year since 2014, and those changes have saved her over $8,000. Last year 65% of our clients changed plans at their review and saved an average of $2,002.

Step 4: Pick the right supplement letter, then the right company. Supplement plans differ by coverage and by cost, and the insurance places usually show you the plans that pay them the most. Janice came to us for a second opinion after an insurance person made a recommendation, and the letter that actually fit her saved her over $100 a month, $1,200 in her first year. Then we look at the company itself: its rating, its rate-hike history, its loss ratio and its service. Most people keep that company for life.

Step 5: Make it official, so your enrollment, your paperwork and any high-income adjustment are all done right.

[ON SCREEN: "The healthiest people when they join tend to regret their decision the most."]

And it's funny, but the people who are healthiest when they first join Medicare are the ones who tend to regret it the most, because they didn't think about the long run.
